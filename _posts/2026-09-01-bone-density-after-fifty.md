Walking is good for you. I will never tell a client otherwise. But if your goal is to hold on to bone mass through your fifties, sixties and beyond, walking alone is not enough, and it helps to understand why.

Bone is living tissue. It lays down new mineral in response to mechanical stress, and it does so most readily where that stress is greatest. Walking loads the hips and spine gently and repetitively. Bone adapts to that quickly, then plateaus.

<!--more-->

## Progressive load is the signal

What bone needs is a load it has not felt before, applied safely, then increased over time. That is precisely what the apparatus provides. The Reformer, the Wunda Chair and the Cadillac all use springs, and springs can be adjusted in small, controlled steps from one session to the next.

Two examples from the repertoire:

- **Footwork on the Reformer** loads the hips and femurs through a long, controlled range, with the spine supported on the carriage.
- **Standing work on the Wunda Chair** loads the spine vertically, in the direction gravity does, but with the resistance dialled to exactly what you can control.

## What we avoid

An osteoporosis-aware programme leaves out loaded spinal flexion and loaded rotation, the movements that put the front of the vertebrae under compression. That means some of the classical mat repertoire is modified or removed. It does not mean the work is gentle. It means it is specific.

## The other half of the equation

Bone density is one reason to train after fifty. Balance is the other. Most fractures follow a fall, and the single best protection is a body that can catch itself. Single-leg work on the apparatus, done with the spring tension turned up, trains exactly that reflex.

If you have a DEXA scan, bring it to your assessment. If you do not, ask your physician for one. The programme will be better for it.
