Most studios will put you on a Reformer within five minutes of walking in. I understand the impulse. People arrive wanting to move, and it feels generous to let them.

I do it differently, and the reason is simple. I cannot write a programme for a body I have not looked at.

## What the assessment actually covers

The first half hour is not exercise. It is a conversation and a series of quiet observations.

- **Standing posture.** Where the weight sits in the feet, whether one hip rides higher, how the head sits over the shoulders.
- **Gait.** A few lengths of the studio. Walking tells me more about hip and ankle control than any test on the apparatus.
- **Range of motion.** Spinal flexion, extension and rotation, hip and shoulder range, and, more importantly, the quality of the movement at the end of that range.
- **History.** Surgeries, injuries, the sport you played at nineteen, the desk you sit at now. All of it shows up in the body eventually.

<!--more-->

## Then we move

The remaining hour is a real session, but a diagnostic one. I will put you on three or four pieces of apparatus and watch how you respond to spring resistance. The Reformer footwork alone, done slowly, reveals whether the knees track, whether the pelvis holds, and whether you breathe or brace.

## What you leave with

A written programme. Not a printout of exercises, but a page that says what we found, what we are going to prioritise for the first ten sessions, and why. You will see it change over time, which is the point.

Joseph Pilates wrote that in ten sessions you feel the difference. Those ten sessions are far more effective when the first one was spent looking properly.
